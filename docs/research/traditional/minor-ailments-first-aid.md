# Minor ailments and first aid: traditional and folk remedies

Household first-aid folklore (burns, stings, hiccups, ears), separating the useful from the harmful.

## Burns

### Butter, oil or goose fat on burns
- **Use:** Kitchen burns and scalds.
- **What it is / how it's used:** An old European and North American tradition (popularised by a 19th-century surgeon). Smear butter, lard or oil on the burn.
- **Evidence:** Disproven / likely harmful.
- **Safety:** **Harmful. Fats trap heat, don't cool the skin, and raise infection risk.** NHS: do not put creams, oils or butter on burns.
- **Sources:** [NHS: Burns and scalds](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/burns-and-scalds/), [British Red Cross: First aid for burns](https://www.redcross.org.uk/first-aid/learn-first-aid/burns), [UT Austin Think Twice: Butter for burns](https://sites.utexas.edu/think-twice/?p=1616)
- **HealthHub fit:** First-aid quick card: "20 minutes cool running water", with a built-in 20-minute timer.

### Toothpaste, egg white, ice on burns
- **Use:** Burns.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Common modern household myths in Europe, Asia and the Americas.
- **Evidence:** Disproven / likely harmful.
- **Safety:** **Toothpaste and creams don't cool and contaminate the burn. Ice damages tissue further.** Cool or lukewarm running water for 20 minutes, within 3 hours, then cover loosely with cling film.
- **Sources:** [British Red Cross: Burns](https://www.redcross.org.uk/first-aid/learn-first-aid/burns), [Scripps: 7 common first-aid myths](https://www.scripps.org/news_items/5354-7-common-first-aid-myths-about-burns-sprains-cuts-and-more)
- **HealthHub fit:** Myth list in first aid.

### Aloe vera gel (straight from the plant)
- **Use:** Minor burns, sunburn.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Ancient Egyptian, Mediterranean, Caribbean and Latin American tradition. Split a leaf and apply the gel after the burn has been cooled.
- **Evidence:** Promising. A 2024 meta-analysis of 9 RCTs found second-degree burns healed about 3.8 days faster than with other dressings (low-certainty evidence). No proven pain benefit.
- **Safety:** Low risk topically. Use only after cooling, and not on deep burns. Rare allergy. **Do not take aloe latex by mouth (it is a strong laxative).**
- **Sources:** [2024 aloe burn meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38605441/), [Cochrane: Aloe vera for wounds](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22336851/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Household plant/remedy list. Sunburn after-care routine.

### Honey on burns
- **Use:** Minor (partial-thickness) burns and wounds.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Ancient Egyptian, Greek, Ayurvedic and African wound tradition. Today, sterile medical-grade honey dressings.
- **Evidence:** Promising / Proven for partial-thickness burns. Cochrane found honey heals them about 4-5 days faster than conventional dressings (high-quality evidence for that comparison).
- **Safety:** Use medical-grade sterile honey, not the kitchen jar, on open wounds. Not on infants under 1.
- **Sources:** [Cochrane: Honey as a topical treatment for wounds](https://www.cochrane.org/CD005083)
- **HealthHub fit:** First-aid kit suggestion (medical honey dressings).

## Stings

### Urine on jellyfish stings
- **Use:** Jellyfish stings.
- **What it is / how it's used:** A global beach myth.
- **Evidence:** Disproven / likely harmful. A 2023 Cochrane review found no trials. Urine can act like fresh water and trigger more venom release.
- **Safety:** Don't. Rinse with seawater, remove tentacles, and soak in hot water (about 45 °C) to ease pain. Vinegar is useful mainly for box jellyfish.
- **Sources:** [Cleveland Clinic: Should you pee on a jellyfish sting?](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/pee-jellyfish-sting), [HMRI: urine not recommended](https://hmri.org.au/how-treat-jellyfish-stings-hint-urine-not-recommended/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Travel first-aid card.

### Hot water and vinegar for jellyfish
- **Use:** Jellyfish stings.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Australian and Pacific practice: vinegar for box jellyfish, hot-water immersion for pain.
- **Evidence:** Promising (low-certainty evidence for hot water). Vinegar inactivates stinging cells in the lab for some species, but can worsen others.
- **Safety:** Test the water temperature so it doesn't scald. Seek emergency care for box jellyfish stings.
- **Sources:** [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/pee-jellyfish-sting), [HMRI](https://hmri.org.au/how-treat-jellyfish-stings-hint-urine-not-recommended/)

## Hiccups

### Sugar, cold water, lemon, vinegar, breath holding
- **Use:** Hiccups.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Global grandmother tricks: swallow a teaspoon of granulated sugar, sip ice-cold water, bite a lemon, taste vinegar, hold your breath, pull your knees to your chest, breathe into a paper bag.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal. NHS lists these tricks as things that may help, though they are not proven.
- **Safety:** Low risk. Never put a plastic bag or any bag over the head. See a GP for hiccups lasting more than 48 hours.
- **Sources:** [NHS: Hiccups](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/hiccups/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Fun "try these" card. A good example of a harmless old wives' tale.

## Ears

### Ear candling
- **Use:** Earwax, ear pain.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Marketed as "Hopi" or ancient practice.
- **Evidence:** Disproven / likely harmful.
- **Safety:** **Burns, perforated eardrums and blockages have been reported (FDA).**
- **Sources:** [FDA: Stay away from ear candles](https://drugs.com/fda-consumer/don-t-get-burned-stay-away-from-ear-candles-125.html)
- **HealthHub fit:** Red card.

## Nausea and travel sickness

### Ginger for travel sickness
- **Use:** Car and sea sickness.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Asian tradition, also used by sailors. Ginger sweets or capsules before travel.
- **Evidence:** Promising (good evidence in pregnancy nausea; less for motion sickness).
- **Safety:** Low risk.
- **Sources:** [Viljoen 2014 meta-analysis (PMC3995184)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3995184)

## Muscle aches and bruises (folk bodywork)

### Cupping (koppning, banki, hijama)
- **Use:** Aches, "drawing out" colds.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Chinese, Middle Eastern (hijama, wet cupping), Russian (banki) and old Nordic practice (koppning, done by "kopparkäringar"). Heated or suction cups are placed on the skin.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal. Mostly low-quality research, with possible pain relief.
- **Safety:** Burns, scarring, infection. Bloodborne disease from unsterilised wet-cupping equipment. Can worsen eczema or psoriasis.
- **Sources:** [NCCIH: Cupping](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/cupping)
