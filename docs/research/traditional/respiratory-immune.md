# Respiratory and immune: traditional and folk remedies

Grandmother remedies for colds, coughs, sore throats, congestion and allergies from Swedish "huskurer" and other traditions worldwide, each checked against the evidence we could find.

## Cough

### Honey for cough (and "varm mjölk med honung")
- **Use:** Acute cough from colds, especially night-time cough in children; sore throat.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Found almost everywhere: Nordic, European, Middle Eastern, Ayurvedic and African home medicine. Give 1/2 to 2 teaspoons of honey neat before bed, or stir it into warm (not boiling) water, tea or milk. The Swedish classic is warm milk with honey.
- **Evidence:** Promising. A Cochrane review found honey probably relieves cough and improves children's night-time sleep better than no treatment, and works about as well as dextromethorphan. The evidence is low to moderate certainty, mostly from single-night studies, and shows no clear benefit after 3 days.
- **Safety:** **Never give honey to babies under 1 year (risk of infant botulism)**, and that includes honey in baked goods or drinks. Honey is sugar, so brush teeth afterwards. Otherwise low risk.
- **Sources:** [Cochrane: Honey for acute cough in children](https://www.cochrane.org/CD007094/ARI_honey-childrens-coughs), [PubMed 29633783](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29633783/), [Harvard Health: Got a cold? Try some honey](https://www.health.harvard.edu/staying-healthy/got-a-cold-try-some-honey), [Svenska bin: honung mot rethosta](https://www.mynewsdesk.com/se/svenska-bin/news/hosta-inte-bort-julen-saa-hjaelper-honung-mot-rethosta-och-halsont-287181)
- **HealthHub fit:** Remedy library entry plus recipe ("honey-lemon drink"). Household profiles should show an age-gate warning when a member is under 1.

### Onion syrup (löksirap, sirop d'oignon)
- **Use:** Cough, chest congestion.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Made across Europe (Sweden, France, Germany, Poland) and in Latin America. Layer sliced onion with sugar or honey in a jar and leave it 6-12 hours. Take a teaspoon of the syrup that forms several times a day.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal. No clinical trials of onion syrup exist. Any benefit probably comes from the honey or sugar (demulcent effect). Swedish paediatrician Göran Wennergren calls onion cough remedies a "skröna" (old wives' tale).
- **Safety:** Low risk for adults and older children. **If made with honey, not for children under 1.** It is high in sugar.
- **Sources:** [Allergia 2019: Wennergren om lök och hosta](https://ebooks.exakta.se/allergia/2019/1905/page_25.html), [People's Pharmacy: onion cough remedy (reader reports)](https://www.peoplespharmacy.com/2013/02/18/onion-cough-remedy/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Recipe card in the remedy library tagged "old wives' tale, harmless". A good place to explain that the honey does the work.

### Cut onion by the bed / onion in hot water
- **Use:** Night cough, blocked nose in children.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Swedish and pan-European folk practice. Put a halved onion on a plate on the bedside table, or in a bowl of hot water, so the fumes "draw out" the cough. Some versions put onion slices in socks.
- **Evidence:** Disproven / likely harmful (in the sense of no effect). Experts say nothing suggests it works. It is a skröna.
- **Safety:** Harmless if the bowl is out of a child's reach (risk of scalds from hot water). It does not replace care for breathing difficulty.
- **Sources:** [Allergia 2019: Wennergren](https://ebooks.exakta.se/allergia/2019/1905/page_25.html), [TV4: Experten sågar populära husmorsknepet](https://www.tv4.se/artikel/5eRuLoG4kS3fB9EMZAwInz/experten-sagar-populaera-husmorsknepet-mot-foerkylning)
- **HealthHub fit:** "Myth or medicine?" card in the remedy library. A fun, harmless tale the owner explicitly wants included.

### Hot lemon and honey drink
- **Use:** Cough, sore throat during colds.
- **What it is / how it's used:** British and Nordic household staple. Half a lemon plus 1-2 teaspoons of honey in hot water, sipped warm.
- **Evidence:** Promising (because of the honey). NHS lists it as a self-care option for cough.
- **Safety:** Not for babies under 1 (honey). Don't give hot drinks to small children (scald risk).
- **Sources:** [NHS: Cough](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/cough/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Recipe in the recipes feature with a "sick-day" tag.

### Pelargonium (umckaloabo)
- **Use:** Cough, acute bronchitis, colds.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Root of *Pelargonium sidoides*, a traditional Zulu and Xhosa (South African) remedy, now sold in Europe as syrup or drops.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal to Promising. NHS says the evidence that it eases cough is limited.
- **Safety:** NHS says it is suitable only for ages 12 and over. Liver reactions have been reported in rare cases, so avoid it with liver disease.
- **Sources:** [NHS: Cough](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/cough/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Remedy library entry with an age limit field.

### Vicks / camphor chest rub, including "Vicks on the feet"
- **Use:** Cough and congestion. The folk version rubs it on the soles and puts socks on.
- **What it is / how it's used:** A 20th-century American household tradition, now global. A camphor, menthol and eucalyptus ointment rubbed on chest or neck. The soles-of-the-feet variant is pure folklore.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal for the feet trick. Chest rub may make the airways feel more open, but one clinical review says there is no data showing it works and that it can increase airflow resistance.
- **Safety:** **Harmful for children under 2. Camphor is highly toxic if swallowed (a few teaspoons can kill a toddler), and a case of respiratory distress has been reported after application in an 18-month-old.** Never put it in or under the nostrils, and never heat it.
- **Sources:** [Mayo Clinic: Vicks VapoRub](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/common-cold/expert-answers/nasal-decongestant/faq-20058569), [Missouri Poison Center: VapoRub](https://missouripoisoncenter.org/is-this-a-poison/vaporub/), [UMEM Educational Pearl](https://umem.org/educational_pearls/836)
- **HealthHub fit:** Remedy library with an age-based red warning. Household medicine-cabinet tracker could flag camphor products.

## Sore throat

### Salt-water gargle
- **Use:** Sore throat. Possibly also preventing colds.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Used worldwide (European, Indian, Middle Eastern, Latin American). About 1/2 to 1 teaspoon of salt in a glass of warm water. Gargle and spit, several times a day.
- **Evidence:** Promising. NHS recommends it for sore throat. A Japanese RCT found that gargling plain water reduced colds (HR 0.60). A pilot RCT of salt-water gargling plus nasal rinsing shortened colds and reduced spread to household members. A small double-blind COVID-19 trial was also positive.
- **Safety:** Low risk. Not suitable for young children who cannot gargle and may swallow it (NHS says "not for children").
- **Sources:** [NHS: Common cold](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/common-cold/), [Prevention of URTIs by gargling: RCT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16242593/), [ELVIS pilot trial (PMC6355924)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6355924), [Saline gargling and nasal rinsing in SARS-CoV-2 (PMC11683512)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11683512/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Routine ("gargle 3x/day while sick") with a checkbox in routines. Possibly a winter prevention habit.

### Plain-water gargling for prevention (Japanese "ugai")
- **Use:** Preventing colds.
- **What it is / how it's used:** A daily habit in Japan, especially after coming home. Gargle tap water about 3 times for 15 seconds.
- **Evidence:** Promising. In an RCT of 387 adults, water gargling reduced upper respiratory infections by about 36%. Iodine gargle was no better than water.
- **Safety:** Low risk.
- **Sources:** [Prevention of URTIs by gargling: RCT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16242593/)
- **HealthHub fit:** "Coming home" habit in routines, alongside hand washing.

## Colds and flu

### Chicken soup ("Jewish penicillin")
- **Use:** Colds and flu symptoms, comfort, hydration.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Eastern European Jewish tradition, also found in Chinese, Latin American (caldo de pollo) and Nordic cooking. Home-made soup with chicken, onion, carrots and root vegetables.
- **Evidence:** Promising (lab only). A University of Nebraska study found that chicken soup inhibited neutrophil movement in a dish, suggesting a mild anti-inflammatory effect. It was an in-vitro study, not a clinical trial. Warm fluids help with hydration and mucus flow.
- **Safety:** Low risk. Watch the salt if someone has high blood pressure.
- **Sources:** [UNMC: Mother approved, doctor tested](https://www.unmc.edu/newsroom/2000/10/17/mother-approved-doctor-tested/), [ScienceDaily 2000](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2000/10/001018075252.htm)
- **HealthHub fit:** "Sick-day" recipe collection in recipes/meal planner. The planner could switch to a sick-day menu.

### Garlic (raw, in honey, or capsules)
- **Use:** Preventing and treating colds.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Used across many cultures (Swedish vitlök, Mediterranean, Chinese, Ayurvedic). Crushed raw cloves in honey or food, or daily garlic capsules.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal. Cochrane found only one eligible trial: fewer colds with garlic capsules, but no faster recovery. That is too little evidence. NHS says garlic has little evidence.
- **Safety:** Causes breath odour and rash. **Raw garlic held against the skin can cause chemical burns.** High-dose supplements may add to the effect of blood thinners (warfarin) and should be stopped before surgery.
- **Sources:** [Cochrane: Garlic for the common cold](https://www.cochrane.org/CD006206/ARI_garlic-common-cold), [NHS: Common cold](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/common-cold/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Remedy library. Recipe tag "garlic-rich".

### Ginger tea
- **Use:** Colds, chills, sore throat, nausea.
- **What it is / how it's used:** From Asian tradition (Chinese, Indian, Southeast Asian) and now Nordic "ingefärsshot". Fresh sliced ginger simmered in water, often with lemon and honey.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal for colds. It is a warming fluid. There is better evidence for nausea (see nutrition-digestion and womens-health).
- **Safety:** Low risk in food amounts. Large supplement doses may add to the effect of blood thinners.
- **Sources:** [Mayo Clinic News Network: Cold remedies](https://newsnetwork.mayoclinic.org/discussion/cold-remedies-what-works-what-doesnt-what-cant-hurt/), [Helsana: household remedies for flu and colds](https://www.helsana.ch/content/helsana/en/blog/body/illnesses/flu-and-colds-these-household-remedies-can-help.html)
- **HealthHub fit:** Recipe with a "sick-day" tag.

### Hot toddy (varm grogg, Irish/Scottish toddy)
- **Use:** Colds, getting to sleep while ill.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Scottish, Irish and Nordic tradition. Whisky or rum, hot water, honey and lemon, drunk at bedtime.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal. Warm fluid and honey may soothe the throat. The alcohol has no benefit and dehydrates.
- **Safety:** **Never for children**, in pregnancy, with liver disease, or together with paracetamol at high doses or sedatives. Alcohol worsens sleep quality.
- **Sources:** [Mayo Clinic News Network: Cold remedies](https://newsnetwork.mayoclinic.org/discussion/cold-remedies-what-works-what-doesnt-what-cant-hurt/), [CSPI: Can at-home remedies ease cold or flu symptoms?](https://www.cspinet.org/article/can-home-remedies-ease-cold-or-flu-symptoms)
- **HealthHub fit:** Remedy library with an alcohol warning. Offer a non-alcoholic version as a recipe.

### Elderberry and elderflower (fläder, holunder)
- **Use:** Flu and cold symptoms. Elderflower tea is used to make you sweat through a fever.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Central and Northern European tradition. Syrup made from cooked black elderberries (*Sambucus nigra*), or tea from dried flowers. Swedish fläderblomssaft is a summer cordial.
- **Evidence:** Promising. A small meta-analysis (4 trials, 180 people) found elderberry reduced upper respiratory symptoms, more clearly for flu. A 2021 review calls the evidence uncertain and finds no sign that it overstimulates the immune system.
- **Safety:** **Raw or unripe berries, leaves and stems contain cyanogenic compounds and cause vomiting, so always cook the berries.** Limited data for pregnancy and children.
- **Sources:** [Hawkins 2019 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30670267/), [2021 systematic review (PMC8026097)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8026097)
- **HealthHub fit:** Seasonal recipe (fläderbärssaft). Remedy library with a "must cook" warning.

### Echinacea (purple coneflower)
- **Use:** Preventing and shortening colds.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Used by Indigenous peoples of the North American Plains. Today it is taken as tea, tincture or tablets.
- **Evidence:** Promising (small effect). It may slightly lower the chance of catching a cold. It is unclear whether it shortens colds.
- **Safety:** Allergic reactions, especially with daisy-family (ragweed) allergy. Limited data for children, pregnancy and drug interactions.
- **Sources:** [NCCIH: Echinacea](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/echinacea)
- **HealthHub fit:** Remedy library. Allergy cross-check against the member's profile.

### Turmeric milk (haldi doodh / "golden milk")
- **Use:** Colds, sore throat, general "immunity", aches.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Indian / Ayurvedic household remedy. About 1/2 teaspoon of turmeric in warm milk, often with black pepper, ginger and honey.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal. Curcumin is anti-inflammatory in the lab, but the amounts used in food are small and poorly absorbed.
- **Safety:** Food amounts are low risk. **Turmeric and curcumin supplements have rare liver-injury reports and can raise INR in people taking warfarin.** Black pepper (piperine) increases absorption of other drugs too.
- **Sources:** [LiverTox: Turmeric](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548561/), [Medsafe NZ: Turmeric early warning](https://medsafe.govt.nz/safety/EWS/2018/Turmeric.asp)
- **HealthHub fit:** Recipe. Show a medication interaction warning when warfarin is in the member's profile.

### Vitamin C from rosehip soup and citrus (nyponsoppa)
- **Use:** "Boosting immunity" against colds.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Swedish nyponsoppa (rosehip soup) and plenty of citrus or C-vitamin during winter.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal for treating colds. NHS says vitamin C has little evidence of helping colds. Rosehip is nutritious food.
- **Safety:** Low risk as food. High-dose vitamin C supplements can upset the stomach and raise kidney-stone risk.
- **Sources:** [NHS: Common cold](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/common-cold/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Swedish recipe (nyponsoppa) in the recipes feature.

### Sauna for colds (bastu / Finnish sauna, Russian banya)
- **Use:** "Sweating out" a cold, preventing respiratory illness.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Finnish, Swedish, Russian and Baltic tradition. Sessions at about 80 °C, alternated with cooling off.
- **Evidence:** Promising for prevention of respiratory illness, based on Finnish observational data. Laukkanen's reviews link regular sauna to fewer respiratory problems and pneumonia. There is no evidence that it helps once you are sick.
- **Safety:** **Avoid with fever, acute illness, alcohol, unstable heart disease, and in early pregnancy. Supervise children.** Drink plenty of fluids.
- **Sources:** [Laukkanen 2018 review, Mayo Clin Proc](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30077204/), [Laukkanen & Kunutsor 2024 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10989710/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Routine or habit tracker ("sauna sessions per week") with an illness lockout.

## Blocked nose and sinuses

### Steam inhalation (ångbad over a bowl, with or without eucalyptus/pine)
- **Use:** Blocked nose, colds, sinusitis.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Used across Europe and Asia. Lean over a bowl of hot water with a towel over the head for about 10 minutes. Some add eucalyptus, pine or chamomile.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal. Cochrane found inconsistent results and not enough evidence to recommend it. NHS suggests steaming up a bathroom instead.
- **Safety:** **Real burn risk. Case series report children scalded by tipped bowls, often needing skin grafts.** Don't do bowl steaming with children. Use a steamy bathroom instead. Eucalyptus and camphor oils are toxic to young children.
- **Sources:** [Cochrane: Heated, humidified air for the common cold](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28849871/), [Steam inhalation scald case series (PMC1472796)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1472796), [Steam inhalation burns series (PMC3381273)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3381273), [NHS: Common cold](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/common-cold/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Remedy library with a child-safety warning. Point to "steam the bathroom" as the safe version.

### Nasal salt-water rinse (jala neti / neti pot)
- **Use:** Blocked nose, sinusitis, hay fever.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Ayurvedic and yogic practice (jala neti). Rinse each nostril with lukewarm salt water.
- **Evidence:** Promising for colds and sinus symptoms (see the gargle pilot above). Widely recommended for chronic sinusitis.
- **Safety:** **Never use straight tap water. Rare but fatal brain infection from *Naegleria fowleri* amoebas has occurred.** Use distilled or sterile water, or tap water boiled for 1 minute and cooled. Clean the device after use.
- **Sources:** [CDC: How to safely rinse sinuses](https://www.cdc.gov/naegleria/prevention/sinus-rinsing.html), [ELVIS pilot trial (PMC6355924)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6355924)
- **HealthHub fit:** Remedy library with a "water safety" checklist step.

## Allergies and hay fever

### Stinging nettle (brännässla)
- **Use:** Hay fever (allergic rhinitis), "spring cleansing". Also a nutritious spring soup.
- **What it is / how it's used:** European and Nordic tradition: nässelsoppa in spring, nettle tea, and freeze-dried leaf capsules.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal to Promising. Two small RCTs showed modest or marginal benefit for hay fever.
- **Safety:** Generally low risk as food. Nettle may have diuretic and blood-pressure effects and can interact with diuretics, lithium and diabetes drugs. Avoid supplement doses in pregnancy.
- **Sources:** [Mittman 1990 RCT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2192379/), [Bakhshaee 2017 RCT (PMC5963652)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5963652)
- **HealthHub fit:** Swedish spring recipe (nässelsoppa). Pollen-season journal prompt.
