# Nutrition and digestion: traditional and folk remedies

Kitchen remedies for stomach upsets, nausea, bloating, IBS and constipation from Nordic, Mediterranean, Asian and Middle Eastern home medicine.

## Diarrhoea

### Blueberry soup (blåbärssoppa) / strained dried-bilberry decoction
- **Use:** Diarrhoea, upset stomach.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Swedish and Finnish warm bilberry soup thickened with potato starch. The Norwegian version simmers dried bilberries and drinks only the liquid, because whole berries add fibre.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal. There are no trials. It gives fluid and energy, and the tannins are astringent.
- **Safety:** Low risk. Sugar can worsen diarrhoea. Use proper oral rehydration solution for real dehydration.
- **Sources:** [Wikipedia: Blåbärssoppa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bl%C3%A5b%C3%A4rssoppa), [Norsk Ukeblad: reader diarrhoea remedy](https://www.klikk.no/produkthjemmesider/norskukeblad/diar-2995207)
- **HealthHub fit:** Swedish recipe with a "sick-day" tag.

### Rice water, salt-sugar water and oral rehydration
- **Use:** Replacing fluids in diarrhoea and vomiting.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Asian rice congee water and Latin American suero casero. Today, WHO's ORS: clean water plus measured sugar and salt.
- **Evidence:** Proven (ORS, plus zinc in children).
- **Safety:** Measure carefully or use pharmacy sachets.
- **Sources:** [WHO: Diarrhoeal disease](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/diarrhoeal-disease)
- **HealthHub fit:** Household kit checklist plus a fluid tracker.

## Nausea

### Ginger (tea, candied, ginger ale)
- **Use:** Nausea from pregnancy, travel or stomach bugs.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Chinese and Ayurvedic medicine, now in European households. Fresh ginger tea, about 1 g a day, or candied ginger.
- **Evidence:** Promising (best evidence is in pregnancy nausea, from meta-analyses of 12 RCTs). Commercial ginger ale usually contains little ginger.
- **Safety:** Low risk at food doses.
- **Sources:** [Viljoen 2014 meta-analysis (PMC3995184)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3995184)
- **HealthHub fit:** Recipe plus a nausea symptom log.

## IBS, cramps and bloating

### Peppermint (tea and enteric-coated oil)
- **Use:** IBS pain, bloating, cramps.
- **What it is / how it's used:** European monastic and folk herb (pepparmynta). Tea after meals, or enteric-coated peppermint-oil capsules.
- **Evidence:** Proven / Promising for IBS (capsules). Meta-analyses show improvement in overall symptoms (NNT about 4) and abdominal pain. The 2022 review rated the evidence very low quality. Tea has not been studied in the same way.
- **Safety:** Heartburn and reflux are common, so avoid with GERD. Keep peppermint oil away from infants' faces (menthol can cause breathing spasms).
- **Sources:** [Ingrosso 2022 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35942669/), [Alammar 2019 meta-analysis (PMC6337770)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6337770)
- **HealthHub fit:** IBS symptom tracker plus a remedy trial ("2-week peppermint test").

### Chamomile tea (kamomillte) for upset stomach
- **Use:** Upset stomach, cramps, restlessness.
- **What it is / how it's used:** European grandmother tea (kamomill, Kamille, manzanilla in Latin America).
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal for digestion. Better studied for sleep (see sleep).
- **Safety:** **Avoid with ragweed or daisy-family allergy.** Theoretical interaction with warfarin.
- **Sources:** [Chamomile meta-analysis (PubMed 31006899)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31006899/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Tea in the recipes feature. Allergy cross-check.

### Fennel and caraway seed tea (fänkål, kummin)
- **Use:** Bloating, gas, after heavy meals.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Middle Eastern, Indian (saunf chewed after meals) and Central European tradition. Steep 1 teaspoon of crushed seeds in hot water.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal. The EMA accepts fennel as a "traditional use" herbal for mild spasms and bloating in adults.
- **Safety:** Fine for adults. Not recommended under 4 years (estragole).
- **Sources:** [EMA assessment report: Foeniculum vulgare](https://www.ema.europa.eu/documents/herbal-report/assessment-report-foeniculum-vulgare-miller_en.pdf)
- **HealthHub fit:** After-dinner tea in recipes.

## Constipation

### Flaxseed (linfrö)
- **Use:** Constipation.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Used since ancient Greece and in Swedish household tradition (linfrö soaked overnight). 1-2 tablespoons a day with plenty of water.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal. NCCIH only notes historical laxative use.
- **Safety:** Don't eat raw or unripe seeds. Take with enough water. May interact with blood thinners. Uncertain in pregnancy.
- **Sources:** [NCCIH: Flaxseed and flaxseed oil](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/flaxseed-and-flaxseed-oil)
- **HealthHub fit:** Breakfast recipe plus a bowel-habit journal.

## "Tonics" and general claims

### Apple cider vinegar (äppelcidervinäger)
- **Use:** Digestion, weight loss, blood sugar, "detox".
- **What it is / how it's used:** A folk remedy popularised in Vermont (US) folk medicine. 1-2 tablespoons diluted in water before meals.
- **Evidence:** Promising for a small drop in fasting glucose (about 8 mg/dL in type 2 diabetes). Weak evidence for weight loss, gut health and "detox".
- **Safety:** **Erodes tooth enamel (pH about 3), so always dilute and drink with a straw. Undiluted vinegar can damage the throat.** Interacts with insulin, diuretics and some blood-pressure drugs (low potassium).
- **Sources:** [American Heart Association: What can apple cider vinegar really do?](https://www.heart.org/en/news/2024/09/11/what-can-apple-cider-vinegar-really-do-for-your-health), [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/exploring-the-health-benefits-of-apple-cider-vinegar)
- **HealthHub fit:** Myth vs evidence card. Medication cross-check.

### Turmeric (gurkmeja)
- **Use:** Digestion, inflammation.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Ayurvedic and South Asian cooking and remedy.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal at food doses.
- **Safety:** Rare liver injury with supplements. Warfarin interaction.
- **Sources:** [LiverTox: Turmeric](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548561/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Recipe tags. Supplement warning.
