# Children and family: traditional and folk remedies

Folk remedies parents and grandparents use for babies and children (fever, colic, teething, tummy upsets), with clear flags for the ones that are dangerous.

## Fever

### Vinegar socks / wet socks ("Essigsocken", kalla strumpor)
- **Use:** Bringing down a fever at night.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Austrian and German folk practice, also found in Scandinavia and the US. Soak cotton socks in cool water or diluted vinegar, wring them out, put them on with dry wool socks on top, and replace them after about 45 minutes.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal. We found no clinical studies.
- **Safety:** Probably harmless for an older child who isn't shivering. Stop if the child shivers or feels cold. NHS advises against sponging or undressing children to cool them, because fever is a natural response. **A baby under 3 months with 38 °C or more needs urgent medical advice.**
- **Sources:** [USC Folklore Archives: Wet socks fever remedy](https://folklore.usc.edu/wet-socks-fever-remedy/), [The Simple Things: vinegar socks](https://www.thesimplethings.com/blog/2016/1/4/home-remedy-vinegar-socks), [NHS: Fever in children](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/fever-in-children/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Remedy library with a "when to seek care" fever checklist that uses the member's age from their profile.

### Sponging, cold baths and alcohol rubs for fever
- **Use:** Cooling a feverish child.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Very common in many cultures: tepid or cold sponging, cold baths, undressing the child. In Latin American and older US practice, children were rubbed with isopropyl or ethanol alcohol.
- **Evidence:** Disproven / likely harmful. NHS says don't undress or sponge children to cool them. **Alcohol rubs can poison a child through the skin and by inhalation.**
- **Safety:** Never use alcohol rubs. Cold baths cause shivering, which raises core temperature. Treat the child's discomfort with fluids and age-appropriate paracetamol or ibuprofen as advised.
- **Sources:** [NHS: Fever in children](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/fever-in-children/)
- **HealthHub fit:** "Don't do this" list in the household fever guide. Fever log in vitals with red-flag prompts (non-blanching rash, stiff neck, drowsiness).

## Cough and colds in children

### Honey age rule
- **Use:** Child cough (see respiratory-immune for the evidence).
- **What it is / how it's used:** A global grandmother remedy. 1/2 to 1 teaspoon at bedtime for children over 1.
- **Evidence:** Promising for over-1s (Cochrane).
- **Safety:** **Under 1 year: never. Risk of infant botulism.** This includes honey dummies, honey in porridge and onion-honey syrup.
- **Sources:** [Cochrane: Honey for acute cough in children](https://www.cochrane.org/CD007094/ARI_honey-childrens-coughs), [NHS: Cough](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/cough/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Household-wide age-gated warning on any recipe or remedy containing honey.

### Camphor / menthol rubs on babies
- **Use:** Blocked noses and coughs in babies.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Very common worldwide, sometimes applied under the nose or on the soles of the feet.
- **Evidence:** Disproven / likely harmful for babies.
- **Safety:** **Not for under-2s. Camphor is highly toxic, and respiratory distress has been reported.** Use saline drops and a humid room instead.
- **Sources:** [Mayo Clinic: Vicks VapoRub](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/common-cold/expert-answers/nasal-decongestant/faq-20058569), [Missouri Poison Center](https://missouripoisoncenter.org/is-this-a-poison/vaporub/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Medicine-cabinet warning tied to household member ages.

### Bowl steam inhalation for children
- **Use:** Blocked nose, croup.
- **What it is / how it's used:** A European tradition: a child leans over hot water under a towel.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal (no proven benefit).
- **Safety:** **Likely harmful. Many scald injuries in children have been reported, often needing skin grafts.** Sit with the child in a steamy bathroom instead.
- **Sources:** [Scald case series (PMC1472796)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1472796), [Cochrane: Heated humidified air](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28849871/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Child-safety callout in the remedy library.

## Colic

### Fennel tea / fennel water (fänkålste)
- **Use:** Infant colic, gas, wind.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Central European, Nordic, Middle Eastern and South Asian tradition. Weak fennel-seed tea given by bottle or spoon, or drunk by the breastfeeding mother.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal. The European Medicines Agency calls the human data on colic very preliminary.
- **Safety:** **The EMA does not recommend fennel tea for children under 4 because of lack of safety data (estragole content).** Two newborns were reported poisoned after their mother drank large amounts of fennel and anise tea. Tea also displaces milk in a baby's diet.
- **Sources:** [EMA assessment report: Foeniculum vulgare](https://www.ema.europa.eu/documents/herbal-report/assessment-report-foeniculum-vulgare-miller_en.pdf), [Fennel safety update (PMC3414240)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3414240/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Remedy library entry flagged "not for babies".

### Star anise tea for colic
- **Use:** Infant colic.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Caribbean and Latin American tradition ("té de anís estrella").
- **Evidence:** Disproven / likely harmful.
- **Safety:** **Dangerous. Infants have had seizures, vomiting and jitteriness. Chinese star anise is often contaminated with toxic Japanese star anise. The FDA warned against star anise teas in 2003, and paediatric authors say it should no longer be given to infants.**
- **Sources:** [Ize-Ludlow 2004, Pediatrics: neurotoxicity in infants](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15492355/), [FDA warns consumers about star anise teas](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14983806)
- **HealthHub fit:** Red "dangerous" card in the remedy library.

### Gripe water, herbal drops and colic remedies
- **Use:** Colic.
- **What it is / how it's used:** A Victorian British tradition (gripe water: dill, fennel, sodium bicarbonate and historically alcohol), plus modern herbal drops.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal. NHS says anti-colic drops, herbal supplements and probiotics are not recommended and have no evidence they help.
- **Safety:** Check the ingredients for alcohol and sugar. Talk to your health visitor.
- **Sources:** [NHS: Colic](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/colic/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Colic crying log (journal) to show to a health visitor, instead of remedies.

## Teething

### Whisky / brandy rubbed on gums
- **Use:** Teething pain.
- **What it is / how it's used:** An old British, Irish, Nordic and North American grandmother trick: dab spirits on the gums.
- **Evidence:** Disproven / likely harmful.
- **Safety:** **Harmful. Even small amounts of alcohol are dangerous for babies (low blood sugar, drowsiness).** The FDA advises against any rubbed-on teething products because they wash away within minutes, and recommends gum massage with a clean finger or a firm rubber teething ring. We found no specific study on whisky, only the general guidance.
- **Sources:** [AAFP: FDA says OTC benzocaine teething products not safe](https://www.aafp.org/about/news/20180530fdabenzocaine)
- **HealthHub fit:** "Grandma said... but don't" myth card in the remedy library.

### Benzocaine gels and homeopathic teething tablets
- **Use:** Teething pain.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Over-the-counter numbing gels, and belladonna-containing homeopathic tablets.
- **Evidence:** Disproven / likely harmful.
- **Safety:** **The FDA warns against benzocaine under age 2 because of methemoglobinemia (more than 400 cases), and against homeopathic teething tablets because they contained inconsistent amounts of toxic belladonna.**
- **Sources:** [AAFP: FDA benzocaine warning](https://www.aafp.org/about/news/20180530fdabenzocaine)
- **HealthHub fit:** Medicine-cabinet warning.

### Amber teething necklaces (bärnstenshalsband)
- **Use:** Teething pain. Supposedly succinic acid is released by body warmth.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Popular in the Baltic and Nordic countries and now worldwide.
- **Evidence:** Disproven / likely harmful. There is no evidence.
- **Safety:** **The FDA warned in 2018 after an 18-month-old was strangled. Choking and strangulation risk.** The AAP advises against them.
- **Sources:** [AAP HealthyChildren: Amber teething necklaces](https://healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/teething-tooth-care/Pages/Amber-Teething-Necklaces.aspx), [CBS: FDA warns about teething jewelry after 18-month-old dies](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/fda-warns-about-teething-jewelry-after-18-month-old-dies)
- **HealthHub fit:** Red card in the remedy library.

## Tummy upsets

### Blueberry soup (blåbärssoppa) and dried-bilberry tea for diarrhoea
- **Use:** Diarrhoea and upset stomach in children and adults.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Swedish, Finnish and Norwegian tradition. Warm blåbärssoppa (bilberries, sugar, potato starch). The Norwegian version simmers dried bilberries and drinks only the strained liquid, because whole berries are high in fibre.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal. Bilberry tannins are astringent, but there are no trials. It offers fluid and energy but is not a proper rehydration solution.
- **Safety:** Low risk. The sugar can worsen osmotic diarrhoea. **Oral rehydration solution is the evidence-based treatment. Watch for dehydration in small children.**
- **Sources:** [Wikipedia: Blåbärssoppa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bl%C3%A5b%C3%A4rssoppa), [Norsk Ukeblad: diaré-råd (reader remedy)](https://www.klikk.no/produkthjemmesider/norskukeblad/diar-2995207), [WHO: Diarrhoeal disease](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/diarrhoeal-disease)
- **HealthHub fit:** Swedish recipe, plus a sick-day fluid tracker for kids (ml per hour).

### Home oral rehydration (salt + sugar water, rice water, "suero casero")
- **Use:** Diarrhoea and vomiting dehydration.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Rice water (kanji) is a South Asian tradition and suero casero a Latin American one. WHO's ORS is the modern, measured version of these folk drinks: clean water, sugar and salt.
- **Evidence:** Proven (WHO-standard ORS plus zinc for 10-14 days in children).
- **Safety:** Measure carefully. Home mixes that are too salty are dangerous for babies, so use pharmacy ORS sachets when possible.
- **Sources:** [WHO: Diarrhoeal disease](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/diarrhoeal-disease)
- **HealthHub fit:** Household sick-day kit checklist (ORS sachets in stock) plus a fluid tracker.

## Ears

### Ear candling (öronljus / Hopi candles)
- **Use:** Earwax, ear infections, "detox".
- **What it is / how it's used:** Marketed as a Hopi or ancient tradition (the Hopi tribe disputes this). A hollow wax cone is lit in the ear canal.
- **Evidence:** Disproven / likely harmful. The FDA found no valid evidence of any benefit.
- **Safety:** **Burns, perforated eardrums and ear canal blockages needing surgery have been reported. Children are at higher risk.**
- **Sources:** [FDA: Don't get burned: stay away from ear candles](https://drugs.com/fda-consumer/don-t-get-burned-stay-away-from-ear-candles-125.html), [Wikipedia: Ear candling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ear_candling)
- **HealthHub fit:** Red card in the remedy library.
