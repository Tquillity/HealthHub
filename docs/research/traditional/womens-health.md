# Women's health: traditional and folk remedies

Traditional remedies for pregnancy, labour, breastfeeding, urinary tract infections and menopause, from Nordic, Middle Eastern, Chinese and other traditions, with pregnancy safety notes.

## Breastfeeding

### Cold cabbage leaves for breast engorgement (kålblad)
- **Use:** Painful, overfull breasts in early breastfeeding.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Used by midwives in Europe, Australia and the Nordic countries. Chill clean green cabbage leaves, place them inside the bra around the breast (not on the nipple) for about 20 minutes, and repeat a few times a day.
- **Evidence:** Promising. A Cochrane review found cold cabbage leaves may reduce pain and hardness compared with routine care (very low certainty). Women reported high satisfaction.
- **Safety:** Low risk. Wash the leaves. Stop if the skin is irritated. Don't overuse once engorgement settles (it may reduce supply, though this is not proven). Seek help for fever or a red wedge (mastitis).
- **Sources:** [Cochrane: Treatments for breast engorgement during lactation](https://www.cochrane.org/CD006946/PREG_treatment-breast-engorgement-overfull-hard-painful-breasts-breastfeeding-women)
- **HealthHub fit:** Postpartum section of cycle/womens features. Remedy library card with a timer.

### Fenugreek for milk supply
- **Use:** Increasing breast milk.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Middle Eastern, North African, Indian and Ethiopian tradition. Seeds as tea, in food or as capsules.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal. NCCIH says it is unclear whether it increases supply, and studies are mixed.
- **Safety:** **Unsafe in pregnancy above food amounts (linked to birth defects).** It can lower blood sugar and gives sweat and urine a maple-syrup smell. Peanut and chickpea allergy may cross-react.
- **Sources:** [NCCIH: Fenugreek](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/fenugreek)

## Pregnancy

### Ginger for morning sickness
- **Use:** Nausea and vomiting in pregnancy.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Chinese, Indian and later European tradition. Ginger tea, crystallised ginger, ginger biscuits, or about 1 g a day in capsules.
- **Evidence:** Promising to Proven for nausea. A meta-analysis of 12 RCTs (1,278 women) found it reduced nausea versus placebo. The effect on vomiting is weaker.
- **Safety:** No safety signal at normal doses in reviews. Large doses may add to the effect of blood thinners.
- **Sources:** [Viljoen 2014 meta-analysis (PMC3995184)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3995184), [Borrelli 2005 systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15802416/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Pregnancy nausea tracker plus a ginger recipe set.

### Liquorice (lakrits / salmiak) in pregnancy
- **Use:** Not a remedy as such. Liquorice root tea is used for coughs and stomach problems, and liquorice is a popular Nordic treat.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Liquorice root tea, sweets, salmiak.
- **Evidence:** Disproven / likely harmful in large amounts.
- **Safety:** **Large amounts (about 250 g a week) in pregnancy are unsafe and linked to delivery before 38 weeks (Finnish data).** Glycyrrhizin raises blood pressure and lowers potassium. Avoid with high blood pressure or heart or kidney disease.
- **Sources:** [NCCIH: Licorice root](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/licorice-root)
- **HealthHub fit:** Pregnancy food warning in the meal planner. Blood-pressure link in vitals.

### Moxibustion for breech baby (Chinese medicine)
- **Use:** Turning a breech baby from about 33-35 weeks.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Traditional Chinese medicine. Burning mugwort (moxa) sticks are held near the acupoint BL67 at the outer corner of the little toe.
- **Evidence:** Promising. Cochrane says it probably reduces breech at birth when added to usual care (moderate certainty), but makes little or no difference to caesarean rates.
- **Safety:** Burn and smoke risk. Do it only with a trained practitioner and alongside midwife care.
- **Sources:** [Cochrane: Cephalic version by moxibustion for breech presentation](https://www.cochrane.org/CD003928)
- **HealthHub fit:** Pregnancy journal entry. Discuss with the midwife.

## Labour

### Raspberry leaf tea (hallonbladste)
- **Use:** "Toning the womb" and shortening labour in late pregnancy.
- **What it is / how it's used:** British, North American and Nordic midwifery folk tradition. 1-3 cups a day from about 32 weeks.
- **Evidence:** Traditional / anecdotal. Small Australian trials found no significant effect. The UK Committee on Toxicity found no safety concern in the limited data, and the EMA does not recommend it in pregnancy or breastfeeding.
- **Safety:** Typical UK intake can be 4 times higher than the doses that were tested. Discuss with your midwife.
- **Sources:** [UK COT statement on raspberry leaf tea](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/raspberry-leaf-tea/statement-on-the-potential-health-effects-of-raspberry-leaf-tea-in-the-maternal-diet)
- **HealthHub fit:** Pregnancy remedy library card.

### Eating dates in late pregnancy
- **Use:** Easier labour.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Middle Eastern and Islamic tradition, from the Qur'anic story of Maryam. About 6 dates (60-80 g) a day for the last 4 weeks.
- **Evidence:** Promising. Meta-analyses (48 studies in 2024) link dates to shorter labour and less induction, but the included studies are of low quality.
- **Safety:** Low risk. Watch the sugar load with gestational diabetes.
- **Sources:** [2024 dose-response meta-analysis (PMC10759543)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10759543), [Al-Kuran 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21280989/)
- **HealthHub fit:** Third-trimester snack routine plus a recipe. Gestational-diabetes flag.

### Castor oil to start labour
- **Use:** Inducing labour.
- **What it is / how it's used:** An old European and American midwife tradition. A dose of castor oil, sometimes mixed in juice.
- **Evidence:** Disproven / likely harmful (no proof of benefit). Cochrane: three small trials give too little evidence. Every woman who took it felt nauseous.
- **Safety:** Causes diarrhoea, dehydration and cramps. **Don't take it without medical supervision.**
- **Sources:** [Cochrane: Castor oil for induction of labour](https://www.cochrane.org/CD003099)
- **HealthHub fit:** Myth card in the pregnancy library.

## Urinary tract

### Cranberry juice or capsules for recurrent UTI (tranbär)
- **Use:** Preventing recurrent bladder infections.
- **What it is / how it's used:** A North American Indigenous berry, now a global remedy. Daily unsweetened juice or PAC capsules.
- **Evidence:** Proven (for prevention in certain groups). The 2023 Cochrane review found it probably reduces symptomatic UTIs in women with recurrent UTI (RR 0.74), in children, and after bladder procedures. It does not help in pregnant women, care-home residents or neurogenic bladder. It does not treat an active infection.
- **Safety:** Low risk. Juice contains a lot of sugar. Possible interaction with warfarin, so ask your doctor.
- **Sources:** [Cochrane 2023: Cranberries for preventing UTIs](https://www.cochrane.org/CD001321/RENAL_cranberries-preventing-urinary-tract-infections), [PubMed 37947276](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37947276/)
- **HealthHub fit:** UTI log in the cycle feature plus a prevention routine.

### Lingonberry (lingon) for UTI
- **Use:** Preventing bladder infections.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Nordic tradition: lingonsylt, lingondricka and juice concentrate.
- **Evidence:** Promising. A Finnish RCT (150 women) using cranberry plus lingonberry concentrate found UTI recurrence of 16% versus 36% in controls. The trial was open-label and stopped early.
- **Safety:** Low risk. Watch the sugar in sylt and dricka.
- **Sources:** [Kontiokari 2001, BMJ](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11431298/), [AAFP summary](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2002/0201/p490.html)
- **HealthHub fit:** Swedish recipes (unsweetened lingon) in the recipes feature, linked to the UTI log.

## Menopause

### Black cohosh
- **Use:** Hot flushes and menopausal symptoms.
- **What it is / how it's used:** Native American (Indigenous Eastern North American) remedy, now sold as standardised extracts.
- **Evidence:** Promising. A 2023 review of 22 studies found it may help overall symptoms and hot flushes, but not mood.
- **Safety:** **Rare serious liver injury reported.** Watch for dark urine and tiredness. Not in pregnancy. Check interactions.
- **Sources:** [NCCIH: Black cohosh](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/black-cohosh)
- **HealthHub fit:** Menopause symptom tracker in the cycle feature.

## Medication interaction warning

### St John's wort (johannesört) and the contraceptive pill
- **Use:** Low mood (a European folk herb, picked at midsummer).
- **What it is / how it's used:** Tea or tablets.
- **Evidence:** See mental-health-stress.
- **Safety:** **Weakens birth control pills (risk of unplanned pregnancy), warfarin, transplant drugs, HIV drugs and many others. Risk of serotonin syndrome with antidepressants. May be unsafe in pregnancy.**
- **Sources:** [NCCIH: St. John's wort](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/st-johns-wort)
- **HealthHub fit:** Interaction warning in the cycle feature when contraception is logged.
